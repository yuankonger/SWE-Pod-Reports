# Compose the seed company from three demos

No public repository matches the seed company's four-language, four-service shape, so the foundation should be a composite of three permissively licensed demo systems, plus two billing platforms for domain rules. Take per-service contract ownership, uniform task names and the in-memory broker default from the AWS Retail Store sample; take service size (roughly 250 to 900 lines) from Online Boutique; take the "orders publish, billing and notifications consume" topology from the OpenTelemetry Demo and dotnet/eShop. Take billing rules and event shapes from Kill Bill (Apache-2.0) and, as concepts only, Lago (AGPL-3.0). A catalogue of 43 verified contract changes from these repositories' history shows that the consumers people forget are rarely the ones compiled against the schema. They are configuration, flags, proxy templates, user-interface paths, dashboards and consumers that misread a field's unit. No schema-compatibility check caught any of the 15 cases where a consumer was missed. Published research agrees: structural breaks are mechanically detectable, while semantic ones (units, defaults, ordering, the meaning of zero) cause the large incidents and are under-recognised by practitioners. The closest prior benchmark, CooperBench, found messaging did not raise success (17.14% with, 17.64% without), and equal-budget studies mostly find a single agent matches or beats a team. Every task therefore needs a proof that the no-messaging team lacks information it requires, and the headline metric should be the gap to a single agent holding all four services, not a team win.

Two limits apply throughout. Nothing was built or run, so build weight comes from each project's own documentation and wall-clock build times are unknown. Line counts are raw approximate counts by file extension, not `cloc` figures.

Each of the three active polyglot systems contributes something different. **AWS Retail Store** is the best structural model for an environment where each agent owns one service: every contract has one owner, the consumer's regeneration of a client is an explicit cross-service action, and every service exposes the same task names (`test`, `test:integration`, `serve`, `container`) whatever its language ([developer guide](https://github.com/aws-containers/retail-store-sample-app/blob/main/DEVELOPER_GUIDE.md)). It already has Orders in Java and Checkout in TypeScript, but nothing in C# or Python. **Online Boutique** has the smallest and most imitable services, including a 280-line Python email service and an 868-line C# cart, but its root-level proto has no single owner, its test coverage is thin (only Go services and the C# cart have tests), and it runs only on Kubernetes ([source tree](https://github.com/GoogleCloudPlatform/microservices-demo/tree/main/src), [development guide](https://github.com/GoogleCloudPlatform/microservices-demo/blob/main/docs/development-guide.md)). **The OpenTelemetry Demo** is the closest role match: TypeScript storefront, a C# accounting service consuming an order event, and a 102-line email service. It is a poor size model as a whole ([source tree](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/tree/main/src)).

dotnet/eShop is the model for Billing's internals and for implicit contracts. Its `OrderStatusChangedToPaidIntegrationEvent` exists as separate hand-copied classes in four projects, so a publisher-side rename compiles everywhere and fails only at runtime ([eShop source](https://github.com/dotnet/eShop/tree/main/src)). FTGO is the only system found with consumer-driven contract tests as first-class modules, which is worth reading even though the default branch is stale.

### Billing and notification platforms supply the domain rules

The demos have no subscription billing, so domain rules come from billing platforms. **Kill Bill** (Apache-2.0, Java, active since 2012) documents catalog, subscription, typed invoice items (RECURRING, FIXED, USAGE, TAX, REPAIR_ADJ, ITEM_ADJ, CREDIT_ADJ, CBA_ADJ), proration, plan-change timing, and an overdue state machine with default payment retries eight days apart, three times ([Kill Bill subscription guide](https://docs.killbill.io/latest/userguide_subscription)). **Lago** is AGPL-3.0, so it is a source of concepts and public API shapes only. Its OpenAPI spec expresses every amount as integer cents plus currency, defines subscription statuses (active, canceled, incomplete, pending, terminated), and its docs give a testable proration example: €100/month upgraded to €200/month on 15 January yields €45.16 and €109.68 ([Lago OpenAPI](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/getlago/lago-openapi/main/openapi.yaml), [upgrades and downgrades](https://getlago.com/docs/guide/subscriptions/upgrades-downgrades.md)). The licence of the separate `lago-openapi` repository was not verified.

Event contracts come in two documented styles. Lago and Hyperswitch send "fat" events that embed the object, while Kill Bill sends "thin" events carrying only ids, which forces a callback to the REST API ([Lago webhook format](https://getlago.com/docs/api-reference/webhooks/format---signature.md), [Kill Bill events](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/killbill/killbill-docs/v3/userguide/tutorials/kill_bill_events.adoc)). All specify at-least-once delivery and expect consumers to deduplicate. For Notifications, Novu's model is the one to imitate: a workflow declares a payload schema and templates read `payload.<field>` directly, so the trigger payload is the contract; under `additionalProperties: false` even an additive upstream field is rejected ([Novu payload](https://docs.novu.co/framework/payload.md)). Novu's core is MIT but its `enterprise/` tree is proprietary; Apprise (BSD-2, Python) and Notifo (MIT, C#) are cleaner permissive references, though their template and dedup behaviour was not examined.

Three licence cautions matter for a "read, do not copy" policy. Lago, Flexprice, Meteroid and listmonk are AGPL-3.0 and Vendure is GPLv3. DeathStarBench's LICENSE file says Apache-2.0 while its README says GPL v2 ([LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/delimitrou/DeathStarBench/master/LICENSE), [README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/delimitrou/DeathStarBench/master/README.md)). µBench is BSD 4-Clause with the advertising clause ([µBench LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/mSvcBench/muBench/main/LICENSE)).

## Forty-three real contract changes show where consumers get forgotten

Four repositories were mined by `git log` on contract paths and every cited thread was read: Online Boutique, the OpenTelemetry Demo, eShop and eShopOnContainers. Of 43 catalogued examples, 38 come from these four. The commonest kinds are field type or shape changes in a shared protobuf, renames of things that are not in the schema at all, and event-schema or delivery-semantics problems on the message bus.

| Kind of change | Representative example | What happened |
|---|---|---|
| Field type change | `Address.zip_code` int32 to string, [OTel PR #587](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/pull/587) | 13 files across TypeScript, Python and Rust. A reviewer's manual purchase through the UI panicked the Rust shipping service, because the frontend's quote path sent no address; load-generator traffic had passed |
| Unit or meaning change | Money type replaced, [Online Boutique commit e20ed32](https://github.com/GoogleCloudPlatform/microservices-demo/commit/e20ed325f187a19a150fa05c0cc15a5af9c7a88c) | Proto landed alone; nine per-service migration commits followed the same day, then a rounding-overflow fix |
| Unit misread by consumers | `Money.nanos`, [OTel issue #3742](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/issues/3742), [#3747](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/issues/3747), [PR #3752](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/pull/3752) | Three consumers in three languages each got the same field wrong differently; a $24.99 product displayed as $33.90 |
| Enum and event schema plus migration | `OrderStatus` class to enum, [eShop PR #151](https://github.com/dotnet/eShop/pull/151) | Reviewers caught a missing DB column, an event that would serialise as int instead of string, and a UI that used the status string as a CSS class |
| REST versioning | [eShop PR #390](https://github.com/dotnet/eShop/pull/390), [PR #680](https://github.com/dotnet/eShop/pull/680) | Mobile client updated a month after the server; hybrid app endpoints missed until a reviewer asked. V2 later added beside an unchanged V1 |

| Delivery semantics | `auto.offset.reset` mismatch, [OTel PR #3505](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/pull/3505) | Two consumers of one topic disagreed; one "silently dropped" early orders. Found through flaky CI |
| Lost events with success signals | [OTel issue #4156](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/issues/4156) | Checkout returned HTTP 200 and a success span while accounting had no log line and no row |

| Orphaned flag | [OTel PR #3873](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/pull/3873) | A revert removed a flag's only reader and broke another service's tests |
| Responsibility move | Recommendations return ids, not products, [Online Boutique commit 0cf418d](https://github.com/GoogleCloudPlatform/microservices-demo/commit/0cf418df99df17663371bf49b918e476ce60445e) | The caller must now call the catalog itself |

Several kinds fall outside the usual add, remove and rename list. These are a protobuf package rename touching 23 files across ten services ([OTel PR #740](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/pull/740)); service-identity renames staged over about 18 PRs with leftovers found afterwards ([OTel issue #1788](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/issues/1788)); a consumer rewritten from Go to .NET behind an unchanged Kafka contract ([OTel PR #1538](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/pull/1538)); drift between duplicated copies of the schema ([Online Boutique issue #814](https://github.com/GoogleCloudPlatform/microservices-demo/issues/814)); and telemetry attribute names as a contract whose consumers are dashboards and tests ([OTel PR #3355](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/pull/3355)). Three kinds have no verified example in this history and must be authored: a permission or scope change, a rate-limit or page-size change, and a deliberate error or status-code change.

**In 15 of the 43 examples a change landed or was proposed with at least one consumer not updated.** Discovery was by a reviewer running the app by hand (4), CI or test failures (3), user or tester bug reports (5), and later code reading or audit (3). None was caught by a schema-compatibility check, and none of the four repositories showed such tooling in the threads read. The 15-of-43 figure is a property of a sample that favoured discussed PRs, not a base rate. The misses cluster where the consumer is not compiled against the schema: environment variables, proxy templates, flags, tests, dashboards, Helm charts in another repository, and UI paths the load generator never exercises. Verification in these projects is overwhelmingly "run the whole demo and click through an order".

Explicit coordination talk in the threads is sparse and informal. The richest written reasoning is a 19-comment eShopOnContainers debate on sharing event types. One side held that "some duplication is desirable" so services stay self-contained; the other that "there IS A REAL AND IMPORTANT dependency between microservices that share integration events" ([eShopOnContainers issue #724](https://github.com/dotnet-architecture/eShopOnContainers/issues/724)). Three backward-compatibility techniques appear and each can seed a task: a default-preserving config value ([OTel PR #3665](https://github.com/open-telemetry/opentelemetry-demo/pull/3665)), a parallel API version ([eShop PR #680](https://github.com/dotnet/eShop/pull/680)), and content negotiation ([Loki PR #9813](https://github.com/grafana/loki/pull/9813)).

One limit matters for task design. Because the demos are monorepos where everything ships together, no thread discussed deploy ordering of an old producer against a new consumer. Mixed-version rollout tasks have to draw on the research literature instead.

## Semantic breaks and unknown consumers dominate the research; teams rarely beat one agent

### Structural breaks are checkable, semantic ones cause the incidents

The most directly relevant study is Lercher et al., 17 interviews in 11 companies on microservice API evolution. All 17 participants treated deletions and renames as breaking, but **only 5 of 17 treated behavioural changes**, such as a timestamp's timezone, as potentially breaking. Fourteen called manual change-impact analysis error-prone, seven said consumers might be unknown, and all 17 notify consumer teams by ad hoc or manual means. For events, old messages still waiting in the queue force consumers to accept old versions after producers migrate ([arXiv 2311.08175](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.08175)).

Fault studies point the same way. Zhang et al. found 63% of 123 distributed-system upgrade failures came from incompatible cross-version interaction; close to 90% were deterministic and all needed no more than three nodes ([SOSP 2021](https://www.cs.purdue.edu/homes/yonglezh/pub/upgrade-sosp21.pdf)). That supports a small simulated company with two versions live at once. Tang et al. studied 120 cross-system interaction failures and concluded the root causes "are not traditional faults like software bugs and misconfigurations, but are discrepancies" between individually correct systems; only 6% of Spark's integration tests cross-test dependent systems ([EuroSys 2023](https://tianyin.github.io/pub/csi-failures.pdf)). Zhou et al. classed 10 of 22 industrial microservice faults as interaction faults "often caused by missing or incorrect coordination", though the sample is small and practitioner-recalled ([IEEE TSE 2021](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10357355)). Their Train Ticket replications translate directly to a subscription retailer: a shared order-status enum whose values differ between library versions, a key not passed to a dependent service, and a cancel racing a refund ([replication README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/FudanSELab/train-ticket-fault-replicate/master/README.md)).

Public postmortems repeat one shape: a producer emits something valid by its own lights and a consumer reads it under an unstated assumption. Mars Climate Orbiter's ground software emitted pound-seconds where a written interface specification required newton-seconds ([MIB report](https://www.dcs.gla.ac.uk/~johnson/Mars/MCO_report.pdf)). Atlassian's deletion API "accepts both site and app identifiers and assumes the input is correct", and one team passed the wrong kind ([post-incident review](https://www.atlassian.com/engineering/post-incident-review-april-2022-outage)). CrowdStrike's template defined 21 fields while the integration code supplied 20, and tests used a wildcard in the 21st ([RCA](https://www.crowdstrike.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Channel-File-291-Incident-Root-Cause-Analysis-08.06.2024.pdf)). Cloudflare's November 2025 outage began when another team's database permission change doubled a generated file past a consumer's hard limit; its remediation is to validate internally generated configuration like user input ([Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/18-november-2025-outage/)).

Prevention practices all make one side's assumptions explicit and machine-checkable, and fix who moves first. Pact has the consumer state the requests it sends and the minimum response it relies on, but scopes itself to "messages ... rather than the behaviour" ([Pact docs](https://docs.pact.io/getting_started/how_pact_works)). Schema-registry compatibility modes determine rollout order: under BACKWARD, consumers upgrade first; under FORWARD, producers first ([Confluent](https://docs.confluent.io/platform/current/schema-registry/fundamentals/schema-evolution.html)). Google's AIP-180 separates source, wire and semantic compatibility, defining the last only by what "most reasonable developers would expect" ([AIP-180](https://google.aip.dev/180)). Expand/contract requires the provider to know every consumer before contracting ([ParallelChange](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/ParallelChange.html)). Two gaps should be stated plainly: no empirical evaluation was found of how much any of these practices reduces incidents, and no repository-mining study of protobuf or Avro schema evolution in the wild was found.

Human-team research supplies a diagnostic rather than a guarantee. Cataldo and Herbsleb found gaps between coordination requirements and actual coordination significantly increased software failures ([IEEE TSE 2013](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10038372)), but a 25-project replication found no statistical relationship between congruence and bugs or churn ([arXiv 2105.08198](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.08198)). Congruence between who needed to talk and who did is worth logging, not optimising.

### The closest benchmark found messaging did not help

No benchmark found gives each agent a private service of a multi-service codebase with hidden contract tests; absence in about 30 searches is not proof. The closest is **CooperBench**: 652 feature-pair tasks from 12 libraries, two agents each seeing only their own feature, hidden tests on the merged result. GPT-5 and Claude Sonnet 4.5 agents reach about 25% in the two-agent setting, roughly 50% below solo, with pooled retention of 0.59. Communication reduced raw merge conflicts but after merge resolution success was **17.14% with messaging against 17.64% without**, while agents spent up to about 20% of steps communicating. Its top failure symptoms were work overlap (33.2%) and divergent architecture (29.7%) ([arXiv 2601.13295](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.13295)). The important caveat is that CooperBench features can be built independently, so messaging was never strictly necessary. AsynCodeBench adds the most reusable scoring idea: an explicit dependency graph with executable checkers per cross-agent dependency, on 19 tasks with 52 dependencies ([arXiv 2609.32662](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32662)).

General coordination benchmarks report the same behavioural gap from different angles: agents do not ask. The Collaboration Tax paper attributes team loss to a four-stage conversational cascade that includes failing to query the partner, and reports the tax falls with model capability ([arXiv 2608.22152](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.22152)). The MAST taxonomy puts "fail to ask for clarification" at 11.65% of failures in its v2 data and found that adding a verification step gave +15.6% absolute ([arXiv 2503.13657v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.13657v2)). HiddenBench's proceedings listing reports 30.1% accuracy for multi-agent teams with distributed information against 80.7% for a single agent with everything; this figure comes from a search summary and should be rechecked before quoting ([PMLR](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/li26ej.html)).

Equal-budget comparisons mostly favour one agent. Under a fixed reasoning-token budget, single agents "consistently match or outperform" multi-agent systems on multi-hop reasoning, and API budget controls have artefacts that inflate apparent team gains ([arXiv 2604.02460](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02460)). Across 260 configurations, team effects ranged from +80.8% on decomposable work to -70.0% on sequential planning ([arXiv 2512.08296](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08296)). With LLM calls fixed, a planner-executor-critic team scored 0.769 against 0.754 for a single agent, not significant at p = 0.80 ([arXiv 2609.04217](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04217)). No equal-token comparison exists for cross-service coding specifically.

Training work is young. MARSHAL uses self-play with turn-level advantage estimation and agent-specific advantage normalisation ([arXiv 2510.15414](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15414v3)). A November 2026 Oxford study found language-model agents form self-consistent arbitrary conventions that fail with other partners, and that more capability does not reliably improve coordination ([arXiv 2611.06409](https://arxiv.org/html/2611.06409v1)). No work was found that trains peer coding agents with partitioned repository views, or that fine-tunes language models against a partner population.

## What to take for the design, the taxonomy and the first tasks

The recommendations in this section are synthesis from the evidence above, not findings from any single source.

### Seed company design

Use four small services, each a few hundred lines to about 1,000, in one repository with a directory per owner. Storefront calls Orders synchronously. Orders publishes order events. Billing consumes them and publishes payment and invoice events. Notifications consumes order and billing events. This is eShop's payment loop shrunk to four nodes, and the OpenTelemetry Demo's checkout-then-Kafka tail.

Follow the Retail Store pattern for contracts: each provider owns an OpenAPI file in its own directory, and each consumer holds a client derived from it. None of the systems inspected has a machine-readable event schema, so the seed company must choose its own. A producer-owned JSON Schema per event with a fat envelope (`event_id`, `event_type`, `occurred_at`, embedded object) follows the Lago and Hyperswitch shape, and makes Notifications' templates depend on Billing's object fields. Keep a few deliberately implicit contracts in the eShop style (shared config names, flag meanings, a status string reused as display text), because that is where history shows consumers get missed.

Adopt uniform task names per service so a grader runs `test` anywhere, and default the broker to in-memory so episodes avoid containers, a 240-second warm-up or Kubernetes. Give Billing integer minor units plus currency, Kill Bill-style typed invoice items, day-count proration with the Lago worked example as a fixture, upgrade-immediately and downgrade-at-period-end timing, a three-parameter dunning loop, and Stripe-style idempotency keys ([Stripe idempotent requests](https://docs.stripe.com/api/idempotent_requests)). Exploit the language split: Stripe notes that static-language SDKs must match the webhook API version to deserialise events, so strict Java and C# consumers fail loudly on drift where Node and Python consumers tolerate it silently ([Stripe webhook versioning](https://docs.stripe.com/webhooks/versioning)).

### Change-type taxonomy

Label each change on axes that already have published definitions: interface kind, change pattern, compatibility kind broken (source, wire or semantic, per AIP-180), who must move first (per the registry compatibility modes), and coordination dependency type from Malone and Crowston (producer/consumer, shared resource, simultaneity, task/subtask) ([Crowston's page](https://crowston.syr.edu/node/54)). No published taxonomy jointly classifies a cross-service change and the communication it requires, so the list below is a composite.

| Change type | Evidence anchor | Tool-detectable? |
|---|---|---|
| Additive field | Online Boutique PR #60; Novu strict schemas reject additions | Yes, but can still break strict consumers |
| Field rename, removal or retype | OTel #587; Online Boutique 2018 commits | Structurally yes; implicit preconditions no |
| Unit, format or meaning change with same shape | Money migration; three `nanos` misreadings; Mars Climate Orbiter | No |
| Enum value added, renamed or re-encoded | eShop #151; Train Ticket F20 | Partly |
| Endpoint or method re-signed, removed or versioned | eShop #390 and #680; Online Boutique RPC removal | Yes |
| Event envelope, dedup key and delivery semantics | eShopOnContainers #667 and #1022; OTel #3505 and #4156 | No |
| Shared config, flag, name or port | OTel #3570, #3665, #3873; Online Boutique env-var rename | No |
| Responsibility moved between services | Online Boutique 0cf418d; eShopOnContainers #146 | No |
| Data migration with persisted or queued old-format data | eShop #151; Lercher on queued messages; Zhang et al. | Partly |
| Cross-service ordering or race | Zhou F1 and F13 (cancel versus refund) | No |
| Cross-service diagnosis with a wrong first suspect | OTel #1800, #4156 | Not applicable |
| Permission, rate-limit, page-size, status-code change | No history found; must be authored. AIP-180 treats a smaller default page size as breaking | Partly |

### First task set

Start from the roughly twenty historical examples that are a few files and a few dozen lines per service, re-themed for the subscription retailer.

| Task | Seed | Why one agent cannot finish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Money moves from decimal to integer cents across all four services | Online Boutique Money migration; OTel `nanos` bugs | One small contract diff, one small change per service, an ordering question, and a rounding rule each owner must apply identically |
| Postal code changes from integer to string | OTel #587 | Orders gains an implicit "address required" precondition on a Storefront path the author's own tests do not exercise |
| Order or subscription status becomes an enum | eShop #151; Train Ticket F20 | Billing must keep string serialisation for persisted events; Notifications or Storefront uses the string for template or styling |
| Event id not preserved, causing duplicate emails | eShopOnContainers #667 | Symptom is in Notifications' dedup log; cause is in the publisher's envelope |
| Event topic name made configurable | OTel #3665 | Three services must agree on a name and a behaviour-preserving default |
| Late-joining consumer drops events | OTel #3505 | Each agent sees only its own config; neither can diagnose alone |
| Billing adds a field to a fat event | Novu `additionalProperties: false` | An additive change breaks a strict consumer |
| Orders API v2 beside v1, then v1 removed | eShop #680; expand/contract | Provider must learn the consumer set before contracting |
| Plan-downgrade timing changes from immediate to period end | Kill Bill and Lago plan-change rules | A business rule that changes what Orders ships and what Notifications says, with no schema change |
| Orders succeed but invoices are missing | OTel #4156 | Producer signals success; consumer signals are simply absent; plant a recent unrelated change as a distractor |

### Design risks the evidence raises

**Messaging may show no benefit.** CooperBench's null result is the main warning. Run two checks on every task before admitting it: the no-messaging team cannot pass because a needed fact (a field name, unit, default or error code) exists only in another agent's service, and the single agent with all four services can pass. Thompson's pooled, sequential and reciprocal ordering predicts the value of messaging should rise across those types; reporting by type makes that testable, though the ordering is cited here through secondary summaries only ([Nickols](https://www.nickols.us/ThompsonPropositions.pdf)).

**The single agent will probably win at first.** Four services of a few hundred lines fit comfortably in one context, which is exactly the condition under which Tran and Kiela predict single-agent dominance. Treat the all-services agent as the upper reference and report the cost of decentralisation. Meter total tokens inside the harness, because provider-side budget controls are unreliable.

**Structural tasks can be solved by tooling, not coordination.** If agents can see a shared schema file or run a breaking-change linter, rename and retype tasks collapse into mechanical work. The history and the incident record both say the coordination content is in semantic and non-schema contracts, so weight tasks there.

**End-to-end scores hide coordination failures and give sparse credit.** Score each cross-service contract separately with deterministic hidden tests, alongside the integration result. Give each agent runnable local tests, so that verification failures, which MAST puts at 21.30% of failures, do not contaminate the coordination signal. Log whether a needed question was asked. Avoid rewarding message volume.

**Self-play will breed private conventions, and training will memorise the codebase.** Evaluate with held-out partner models, and hold out services or procedurally vary contracts for evaluation. A purpose-written codebase avoids the public-repository contamination documented for SWE-bench ([arXiv 2506.12286](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.12286v2)), but only until the policy has trained on it.

**Demo-derived tasks under-represent rollout ordering.** The reference histories ship everything together. Mixed-version windows, queued old-format messages and incomplete contract phases come from Zhang et al., Lercher et al. and the Knight Capital and Google 2020 incidents, and need a harness that can run two versions of a service at once.

## Conclusion

The survey changes the framing of the environment in two ways. First, the interesting contract is mostly not the schema. In the repositories closest to the seed company, schema-typed consumers were usually updated in the same change, and the misses sat in configuration, units, delivery semantics and paths nobody's tests walked. A seed company that puts every contract in a tidy OpenAPI file would be more disciplined than any reference system and would produce tasks that tooling solves. The design should keep a deliberate share of implicit contracts and semantic rules, because those are what force one owner to ask another.

Second, the environment should not be positioned as a test of whether teams beat single agents. The evidence says they generally do not at equal budget, and coding is named as a poor fit. Here the partition of knowledge is imposed, as it is in real organisations, so the measurable quantity is how much of the single-agent ceiling a team recovers and whether training closes that gap. The untested step is the communication-necessity check: until a pilot shows the no-messaging baseline failing on tasks the all-services agent passes, the CooperBench result should be assumed to transfer.
